## What the sources say

- **Start with the people, not the feature.** If a team does not know who will use a service and what they need from it, it cannot build the right thing (GOV.UK, "Learning about users").
- **Early on, find out four things:** who the likely users are and what they are trying to do, how they do it now, what goes wrong for them, and what they need to reach their goal (GOV.UK, "Learning about users").
- **Use evidence that already exists, then talk to people.** Analytics, search logs, support-desk records, and earlier research come first; then interview and watch actual or likely users, and talk to staff who work with them. Anything that does not come from users is an assumption to test (GOV.UK, "Learning about users").
- **Include everyone, especially those who struggle.** Research must cover all kinds of users, not only typical ones, and should focus on people who have trouble with the current service. That includes disabled people and people who use assistive technology, people with limited digital skills or literacy, and people who need help (GOV.UK, "Learning about users" and "Finding participants").
- **Recruit disabled participants deliberately.** Allow up to a month; allow six to eight weeks for less common cognitive disabilities. Ask about access needs when recruiting. Not everyone with an access need calls themselves disabled, so screen by asking about difficulties, not labels. Do not rely on staff or on the same participants every time (GOV.UK, "Finding participants").
- **Respect people in the session.** Let participants use their own assistive technology, ideally where they normally use it. Speak to the participant, not their helper. Ask rather than assume what they can do or how they feel (GOV.UK, "Running research sessions with disabled people").
- **Learn why people avoid the digital route.** Reasons include no access or skills, low confidence, language or literacy, mental health, learning difficulties, and a service that is not accessible. Research where people actually get help, and have an expert, not the user, judge digital skill (GOV.UK, "Understanding users who do not use digital services").
- **Pick methods by question.** Watching what people do is more reliable than asking what they would do. Qualitative methods answer why and how to fix; quantitative ones answer how many. Early work calls for interviews and field studies; design work calls for usability testing (Rohrer).

## How consult uses it

1. Before a need statement is written, consult asks what evidence there is about who uses the thing: data, research, support records, or staff who work with users. "We assume" is recorded as an assumption, not a need.
2. If the spec's audience leaves out disabled people, people with low digital skills, or people who avoid the web, consult asks whether that was a decision or an omission.
3. When there is no evidence, consult offers the smallest research step that would answer the question, such as three short interviews or a look at support emails, and says build may wait on it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a stakeholder's opinion as a user need.
- Researching only with easy-to-reach, confident users.
- Recruiting disabled participants at the last minute, or reusing the same few people.
- Asking people what they would do instead of watching what they do.
- Using a team member, or a model, as a stand-in for a disabled user. Doctor Accessible never does this (principle 7).
